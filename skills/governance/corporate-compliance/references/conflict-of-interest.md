# Conflict of Interest

## Topics Covered
- conflict-of-interest

## Purpose
Establish the methodology for identifying, assessing, and resolving a potential conflict of interest — the point at which a person's private interest could improperly influence, or appear to influence, their corporate duties — before recommending disclosure, recusal, or another mitigation.

## When to Load
Load whenever a request involves a potential conflict of interest — a related-party transaction, a personal relationship affecting a business decision, an outside activity/directorship, or a personal financial interest intersecting with a corporate role — before characterizing the situation or recommending a resolution path.

## Scope
Covers the general methodology for conflict-of-interest identification, declaration, and resolution. Does not cover gifts/entertainment specifically (see `gifts.md`, though gift acceptance is itself a common conflict-of-interest trigger), anti-corruption-specific conflicts involving public officials (see `anti-corruption.md`), or the specific legal validity of a related-party transaction under corporate law (a specialist question beyond this skill's authority).

## Core Concepts
- **A conflict is a situation, not a misdeed**: the mere existence of a conflicting interest (a family member at a vendor, an outside directorship, a personal investment in a counterparty) is not itself wrongdoing — it becomes a compliance concern only when undisclosed or unmanaged, allowing the private interest to actually or apparently influence a corporate decision. The methodology's goal is disclosure and management, not automatic prohibition.
- **Actual vs. apparent conflict**: an actual conflict exists when the private interest could genuinely influence the decision; an apparent conflict exists when a reasonable outside observer could perceive undue influence even absent proof of actual effect. Both warrant disclosure and management — an apparent conflict left unaddressed damages trust and invites scrutiny even where no actual improper influence occurred.
- **Declaration as the foundational control**: the standard mechanism is a proactive declaration by the individual (commonly through a structured, accessible form — electronic, recurring) rather than relying on others to detect the conflict — a compliance program's effectiveness on this topic depends heavily on whether declaration is genuinely encouraged and easy (including through training that reinforces "when in doubt, declare"), not merely nominally available.
- **Recusal from the specific decision, not necessarily from the role**: the standard mitigation for a genuine conflict is recusing the individual from the specific decision/process where the conflict arises — not necessarily removing them from their broader role — proportionality matters: an overly broad response (e.g., unnecessary removal from an unrelated role) can be as much a design failure as an inadequate response.
- **Corporate opportunity doctrine**: an employee/officer who becomes aware of a business opportunity through their corporate role should generally offer it to the company (or the relevant competent area) rather than pursuing it personally, and should decline related economic advantages arising from that role — this is a specific, recognized sub-pattern of conflict-of-interest management distinct from the general disclosure/recusal framework, since here the concern is diversion of a company opportunity, not merely biased decision-making.
- **Ongoing monitoring, not a one-time event**: declared conflicts should be monitored by the compliance function on an ongoing basis (relationships and circumstances change), and the compliance function is responsible for taking corrective action when a conflict is identified — not merely collecting declarations and filing them.

## Decision Points
1. Does the situation involve a private interest (financial, familial, or otherwise personal) that could influence, or appear to influence, a corporate decision the individual is involved in?
2. Has the individual proactively declared the interest, or was it identified through another means (raising a question about declaration-culture effectiveness worth separately noting)?
3. Is this an actual conflict (real potential influence) or an apparent conflict (perception risk without confirmed actual influence) — both require action, but the framing differs.
4. Does this specific fact pattern implicate the corporate-opportunity doctrine (a business opportunity learned through the corporate role) rather than the general decision-bias framework?
5. What is the proportionate mitigation — recusal from the specific decision, a broader role change, or (in a clear, severe case) something further — matched to the actual scope of the conflict, not a default maximal or minimal response?

## Required Facts
- The specific private interest and the specific corporate decision/process it could affect.
- Whether and how the interest was declared.
- Whether the fact pattern is decision-bias-type or corporate-opportunity-type.
- Any prior similar declarations/conflicts for the same individual, for pattern context.

## Required Evidence
- The declaration record (or its absence).
- Documentation of the specific decision/process the interest intersects with.
- The applicable conflict-of-interest policy's specific thresholds/examples, where they narrow or clarify the general framework. `CORPORATE_CONTEXT_REQUIRED`.

## Exceptions
- A disclosed and properly-managed conflict (recusal completed, decision made by an unconflicted party) is a resolved compliance event, not an ongoing concern — do not treat a properly-managed historical conflict as equivalent to an unmanaged current one.
- Some relationships are common enough that a specific corporate policy may pre-clear certain categories (e.g., a spouse working at an unrelated, non-competing company) — verify the specific current policy's thresholds before treating every conceivable relationship as requiring individual case-by-case escalation. `CORPORATE_CONTEXT_REQUIRED`.

## Risk Considerations
- Failing to distinguish actual from apparent conflicts risks either under-reacting to a genuine perception-risk situation or over-reacting to a situation where the actual-influence risk is negligible — both frame the mitigation wrong.
- Treating recusal from a specific decision as insufficient by default, and over-applying broader role changes, can itself create unwarranted career/reputational harm disproportionate to the actual conflict.
- Failing to apply the corporate-opportunity doctrine specifically (treating a diverted-opportunity case as a routine decision-bias case) misses the distinguishing feature that the company itself may have lost a benefit, not merely that a decision was potentially biased.

## Human Escalation Conditions
Escalate for human compliance/legal review whenever: a declared conflict is material (senior role, significant financial interest, or high-value decision); a corporate-opportunity question is in play; a conflict was discovered rather than proactively declared, raising a separate declaration-culture concern; or the appropriate mitigation is genuinely unclear.
